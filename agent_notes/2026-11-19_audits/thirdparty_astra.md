# Would I start a project with Loft?

**I would use the TypeScript renderer for a procedural short, or to build a particular creature for a game. I would choose another foundation for the game itself.** Loft earns its place when a point on a moving body has to mean the same thing to the painter, the animator and the game programmer. The crab can tell a game where a boat is held and how hard it is squeezed, using the same machinery that draws the boat. I could reproduce its silhouette in several engines. Reproducing that agreement through revisions is the expensive part. The counterweight is a deployment problem I could measure on the simplest example. The TypeScript path produced a five-draw lantern. The job prepared for the new Rust compiler contained plenty of mathematics and zero draw declarations. See `examples/bosses/titan/rig.ts:653`, `examples/bosses/titan/rig.ts:699`, `src/cli/main.ts:232` and `src/record/recorder.ts:132`.

## What writing a scene feels like

I would hand a newcomer the paper lantern. Its spine is a point parameterized by height. A half-sine sets the belly radius, a cosine pinches the ribs, and the paint uses the same longitudinal coordinate to darken the paper there. The author can count fourteen ribs in the formula. A strand joins the spine, radius and paint, then a scene supplies the ground, style, sun and named cameras. The swinging example imports the radius and paint unchanged. It defines a pendulum angle as a function of time and locates the rope and the lantern along the same line. Changing the swing does not require an animation update to move each piece separately. This is a small, legible reason to adopt the library. See `examples/first/lantern.ts:7`, `examples/first/lantern.ts:27`, `examples/first/lantern.ts:42` and `examples/first/lantern_swings.ts:11`.

The underlying abstraction survives a less convenient shape. A frame contains an origin and three directions. A surface offers positions and normals, a chart can locate another part, and paint can read world position, normal and copy index as well as surface coordinates. A strand extends a body, whose frame at a body coordinate provides a place to attach detail. A transported station frame depends on its predecessor. The implementation carries that state through a scan, so an author describing a bent tube need not maintain a separate orientation for every ring. This is especially attractive for antennae, vertebrae, feathers, shell ridges and moving attachments. An ordinary mesh with good UVs and a rig already solves much of this for an artist with a finished asset. Loft has the advantage when the number, proportions or motion of the parts are themselves program inputs. See `src/map/types.ts:13`, `src/map/types.ts:68`, `src/map/types.ts:109`, `src/geom/strand.ts:80`, `src/map/sweep.ts:263` and `src/map/transport.ts:135`.

The examples go well beyond variations on a sphere. The drilled bead intersects a ray with a sphere and cylinder, chooses the exposed wall and supplies its normal. Its renderer accepts that hit function plus a bound and paint. The material study constructs two rows of spheres, varies roughness and metal content, and projects a noise gradient into a tangent plane for hammered copper. The dust example goes the other direction. It specifies a 120-step simulation of 2,048 grains, writes compute WGSL and draws instanced camera-facing quads from the results. These examples show three sensible levels of control. They also show where the convenience runs out. A mathematically unusual surface needs somebody who can get the intersection, coordinate conventions and miss case right. A handwritten compute pass needs somebody who understands memory layout and dispatch coverage. See `examples/bead.ts:10`, `examples/bead.ts:30`, `examples/materials/spheres.ts:19` and `examples/compute_steps.ts:3`.

The strongest evidence for building a product here is the heron and the crab. The heron's leap is described by constraints on position, velocity, flight acceleration, foot planting, reach and clearance. The programmer can change the deck height and ask whether the problem still has an acceptable solution. The crab has interchangeable parts and a two-stage assembly that combines its movements with the sea. It exports strikes, holds, waves, game inputs and checks beside the rendered scene. There are 36 TypeScript files in its titan parts directory alone. This is unusually substantial example code. It also tells me what it costs to make another creature at that level. Some of the design machinery is explicitly marine: the general-looking staged design type accepts a sea specification and a function finishing the member on water. A team making mechanical assemblies would get less reuse from that layer. See `examples/second/heron_leap.ts:54`, `examples/second/heron_leap.ts:70`, `examples/bosses/reefback_titan.ts:305`, `examples/bosses/reefback_titan.ts:315` and `src/design.ts:44`.

The entrance is wider than the first example suggests. Importing the root returned **451 runtime names** on this checkout, before counting type-only exports or methods on objects like `draw`. The barrel explicitly resolves collisions including `compose`, `extrude` and `round`. It puts arithmetic, derivative operators, field tracing and compiler extension hooks within reach of an author. Yet the function a browser needs to actually display the result, `createHost`, lives behind an internal path. The bundled viewer itself reaches there. The package is private, version 0.0.1, and exports only the TypeScript source root. I could learn the first scene in an afternoon. Budgeting an afternoon to package, integrate and support that viewer in my own application would be optimistic. See `src/index.ts:3`, `src/index.ts:11`, `src/core.ts:26`, `src/host/index.ts:3`, `viewer/main.ts:7` and `package.json:2`.

## From TypeScript to a frame

A call such as `mul(time, 2)` builds a typed expression. Scalars, vectors and booleans are the basic types. Leaves include host inputs, tunable parameters and table reads. Nodes represent arithmetic, calls and bounded iteration. Constructors intern repeated expressions, using child IDs for common operations. This lets separately authored parts share an expression and gives analysis passes stable handles. The same graph can emit WGSL, build a JavaScript evaluator or yield a symbolic derivative. The JavaScript evaluator generates a function with the Function constructor. A browser application with a policy forbidding dynamic code generation has a concrete reason to compile ahead and consume files. Raw library functions can declare themselves GPU-only, so CPU evaluability depends on what the author called. See `src/expr.ts:13`, `src/expr.ts:56`, `src/expr.ts:169`, `src/expr.ts:217`, `src/expr.ts:542`, `src/expr.ts:1493` and `src/grad.ts:442`.

There is a second way to author mathematics. The tagged WGSL entry accepts interpolations, tracks their positions, checks reserved names and parses a module. Supported functions can lower into the expression system. The lowerer also recognizes calls to raw functions and rejects operations outside a particular lowering context. I like being able to bring shader code into an existing scene. I would teach this route after the graph. A perfectly reasonable question such as “can this expression take part in a clearance check?” now depends on both shader validity and the subset the CPU analysis understands. The tagged module's TypeScript call signature uses `any`; a considerable part of its contract is enforced when the program runs. The text frontend alone is six files and 2,312 physical lines. See `src/text/index.ts:55`, `src/text/index.ts:63`, `src/text/lower.ts:613`, `src/text/lower.ts:709` and `tests/ranged.test.ts:156`.

The TypeScript compiler starts with each drawable's emitted shader modules and draw specifications. It reserves slots, discovers dependencies such as offscreen passes through shader functions, plans tables, seals bindings, constructs shared shader text and orders draws. The phase order includes background, opaque, on-bodies, water, on-water and transparent. Order among emitters in the same phase is preserved by filtering the original list. This explains both the ease of drawing a water scene and a limitation for general compositing. Two intersecting transparent objects do not acquire a camera-dependent sorting solution by being labeled transparent. Pass dependency analysis, on the other hand, can diagnose a cycle and name its participants. See `src/scene.ts:89`, `src/compile/index.ts:51`, `src/compile/index.ts:86`, `src/compile/index.ts:103`, `src/compile/index.ts:163`, `src/compile/context.ts:34` and `src/compile/passes.ts:190`.

A substantial part of the compiler answers “how often must I do this?” It distinguishes build, frame, copy and item rates. It counts readers and estimates the cost of a frame expression before lifting it out of repeated evaluation. Tables have parallel maps, dependent scans, static data, replay and CPU-filled cases. The scheduler detects dependency cycles, packs data against bounded storage bindings and gives GPU stages an order. Three of eight storage-buffer slots are reserved, leaving five for tables under this policy. A scan emits a loop over its steps and hoists invariant expressions before the loop. Independent bodies can provide parallel work, but doubling the length of one dependent spine still increases the work on its serial path. These decisions have direct bearing on authoring. A hundred copies can be inexpensive if they share the right work, and a single elaborate spine can consume disproportionate pose time. See `src/compile/rates.ts:20`, `src/compile/rates.ts:120`, `src/compile/tables.ts:39`, `src/compile/tables.ts:652` and `src/compile/tables.ts:730`.

Lowering produces an explicit description of buffers, textures, bind groups, pipelines, dispatches, per-frame writes and render passes. The folder format separates a manifest, WGSL chunks and binary blobs. This is an excellent boundary for handing an asset to another renderer. The writer refuses a scene whose per-frame tables still require JavaScript. There is a test checking that the loader's import closure excludes the compiler and a second that compares pixels after serializing and reading back. Two qualifications matter for shipping. Rebuilding an existing directory removes its previous shader and binary subdirectories before writing their replacements. A failed or overlapping publication can expose an incomplete generation. Also, the live scene's loop period is absent from the manifest consumed by the CLI; its playback helper defaults to open time. I would stage a generation before making it live, and have the application own playback timing. See `src/host/lower.ts:923`, `src/host/manifest.ts:185`, `src/host/files.ts:28`, `src/cli/main.ts:104` and `tests/files.test.ts:20`.

## What the GPU pays for

Loft mixes several rendering techniques. Generated grids rasterize parameterized surfaces, hull outlines can draw another shell, and the screen-outline option adds an image pass. Fields can be marched within bounds, sampled into bricks, or converted into meshes. Automatic field selection considers variability, estimated evaluation cost and whether the formula reads pixel size. That is an informed heuristic, with meaningful escape hatches. I would still inspect close silhouettes. The field shader explicitly treats exhausting its step budget inside the bound as a hit, then refines its estimate. This trades holes for possible depth or silhouette errors. The CPU distance check samples a field; a steep change between its samples remains possible. The shader's approximation is entirely reasonable for inked art and needs particular scrutiny for geometry used as a measurement. See `src/draw/surface.ts:460`, `src/compile/index.ts:99`, `src/draw/field.ts:159`, `src/draw/field.ts:283` and `src/draw/field.ts:597`.

Opaque triangle count tells only part of the story. A bounding proxy filling half the image can be costly even when its triangle count rounds to nothing on a profiler. The volume marcher samples along the visible ray, skips empty contributions, integrates opacity and can terminate near saturation. Optional shadow marching adds density evaluations along light rays. For a dense medium covering 100,000 pixels, 48 view samples and eight shadow samples per occupied view sample imply roughly 38 million shadow-density evaluations for one light if every sample contributes and no ray exits early. That is a work estimate, not an observed duration. Thin volumes pay less. In the Rust template the nested calls make this dependence especially clear. The TypeScript host also uses four-sample color and depth in its normal scene path and can add a depth resolve and postprocessing textures. Bandwidth and screen coverage are as important as primitive throughput. See `src/draw/volume.ts:183`, `src/draw/volume.ts:216`, `rust/crates/loft-core/wgsl/march.wgsl:5`, `src/host/manifest.ts:257` and `src/host/lower.ts:698`.

I measured Bun 1.3.10, Apple M4, macOS 15.5, Metal, f32, 960 × 540, time zero. Each scene got a fresh Bun process and device; I did not flush OS or driver caches. Author time starts after importing the library and measures importing the example. Compile and lower are timed separately. Load starts with a device and an in-memory bundle. WGSL bytes sum the stored chunks once. Draw counts below are declared by the compiler. GPU time is the host's minimum of 12 normal timed frames after two warmups. Readback is median wall time for 12 calls returning RGBA bytes. These measure different things. In particular, the GPU figure adds pose and draw intervals and excludes the gap between submissions. See `src/host/host.ts:805`, `src/host/host.ts:897` and `src/cli/profile.ts:21`.

Notice that the material study took longer to load than the creature in this run. Pipeline count alone cannot predict load time, and these are single load observations. I would not extrapolate them to mobile, 4K, a moving close camera or a crowded level. The browser path plans with the last available bounds and asynchronously reads fresh ones. The readback path waits for current results and returns pixels. A live canvas benchmark should use the former. The host also has a per-device cache for repeated modules and pipelines, an advantage for a service that reuses a device. ## How much of the Rust engine is available?

There are two materially different Rust integration stories. For scenes compiled by TypeScript, loft-loader models the artifact directory and can encode a frame on a caller's wgpu device. That is a real route into a Rust project and a reason to consider Loft for a single asset. The newer stack has loft-ir for the graph, wire format and bundle, loft-core for compilation and analyses, loft-node for the native binding, loft-gpu for loading and rendering, and loft-draw for headless operation. Its single-file bundle carries a versioned header, JSON and aligned binary sections. The reader checks lengths before section allocation and rejects trailing bytes. The GPU crate depends on the IR and Naga and takes a supplied device and queue. It validates a bundle against device limits before creating GPU objects. These are sensible divisions of ownership. See `rust/crates/loft-loader/src/lib.rs:501`, `rust/crates/loft-loader/src/lib.rs:654`, `rust/crates/loft-ir/src/bundle.rs:275`, `rust/crates/loft-ir/src/bundle.rs:306`, `rust/crates/loft-gpu/Cargo.toml:9` and `rust/crates/loft-gpu/src/lib.rs:440`.

Inside the new compiler, decoding, type checks, scheduling, resource layout and WGSL emission are implemented. The driver distinguishes scene, solve and check jobs. Solve can return unproved holds; check separately attempts proofs. Evaluate still returns an unbuilt-program error. Draw lowering combines imported WGSL templates with generated placement, paint and hit functions for grids, meshes, quads, ray bounds and media. This is substantive graphics work. Some optimization and feature coverage is visibly incomplete. The driver plans lifted frame values and then removes their kernels, resources and references. Its fill-declaration builder handles maps and scans and emits no fill declaration for a recurrence. Those facts keep me from crediting the native compiler with the TypeScript engine's scheduling or animation performance. See `rust/crates/loft-ir/src/wire.rs:901`, `rust/crates/loft-core/src/lib.rs:150`, `rust/crates/loft-core/src/lib.rs:207`, `rust/crates/loft-core/src/lib.rs:563`, `rust/crates/loft-core/src/lib.rs:572` and `rust/crates/loft-core/src/draw/mod.rs:227`.

The decisive gap is at the front door. Selecting the Rust build starts watching TypeScript emission, loads and compiles the ordinary source scene, then packs a job from the observed expression roots. Scene-level passes, checks and solves default to empty. I followed those calls for the first lantern in memory. The result was **1,408 nodes, 80 roots and 40,224 bytes**, with declaration kinds Kernel, Loop, Fn and Param. There was no Draw, Pass or Camera. By comparison, the handwritten sample explicitly passes a scene containing six draws. The Rust lowerer iterates the graph's actual draw declarations. The CLI even has a successful-build reporting branch for zero draws. Loading the installed addon independently stopped at its ABI check: installed ABI 2 / wire 1, expected ABI 5 / wire 2. I did not rebuild it under the one-output-file restriction. The missing declarations were observed upstream of that failure, so a current binary alone would not fix the lantern input. See `src/cli/args.ts:207`, `src/cli/load.ts:33`, `src/cli/main.ts:235`, `src/cli/main.ts:248`, `src/record/sample.ts:27`, `src/record/sample.ts:64` and `src/core/native.ts:77`.

I would keep the new host design. A game can prepare multiple instances, reuse pipelines and draw just the subject into an existing render pass. The code binds per-instance state and issues a draw for each instance and item, so sharing a pipeline does not collapse all instances into a single hardware draw. The host refuses instancing when live steps, offscreen passes or certain view-sized writes would be shared incorrectly. I would rather see that constraint at asset integration time than discover two characters influencing each other's pose. Shipping this API still means owning version compatibility. The workspace pins wgpu and Naga to 30.0.1, its lockfile has 151 package records, and dev dependency builds, lab builds and thin-LTO release builds have different optimization costs. I verified the TypeScript typecheck; I have no clean Cargo build timing. See `rust/crates/loft-gpu/src/lib.rs:1265`, `rust/crates/loft-gpu/src/lib.rs:1367`, `rust/crates/loft-gpu/src/lib.rs:1382`, `rust/Cargo.toml:14`, `rust/Cargo.toml:35` and `tools/dev.sh:45`.

## What I trust, and what I would have to own

The tests that most increase my confidence cross a boundary using an independent observable. The derivative suite checks centered finite differences and separately compares evaluated derivatives on the GPU. Pose tests read back GPU-filled tables for a serpent and a crab and compare them with CPU values. The telegraph test renders water with and without the warning, projects a grid of world points and compares changed pixels with the hit shape, excluding an explicit band at the antialiased edge. The volume test asks how much of an emissive slab lies in front of an occluder. The GLB writer has a test reading its output with a separate parser. These assertions would catch a wrong normal, buffer offset, camera mapping or integration length that a screenshot of a plausible creature might miss. See `tests/grad.fd.test.ts:106`, `tests/pose.gpu.test.ts:38`, `tests/spacetime.test.ts:105`, `tests/volume.gpu.test.ts:23` and `tests/bake.gltf.test.ts:19`.

The distinction between a proof and a successful sample matters. A skin-clearance test expressly expects a sampled verdict and checks the words used to print it. Ranged tests cover strict versus non-strict truth at zero margin, exhaustion of a shared box budget, and constructs the evaluator cannot interpret. The solver tests account for rounded spline controls failing a constraint satisfied in double precision. This gives an author somewhere to go when two subsystems disagree. It still falls to the author to express the right property. In the heron, clearance for parts of the body is stated at a finite list of stations. Proving those predicates over continuous time establishes exactly what they say about those stations. It does not certify every surface point on a detailed bird. See `tests/spacetime.test.ts:76`, `tests/ranged.test.ts:106`, `tests/ranged.test.ts:130`, `tests/solve.test.ts:98` and `examples/second/heron_leap.ts:64`.

I would read a green native test result with its inputs beside it. The Rust instance test prepares two poses, draws into a caller-owned game pass and inspects the resulting pixels. It starts with an explicitly assembled bundle. The Rust draw tests build graphs and cover multiple shader templates. The corpus comparer, however, iterates over an empty frame list when a scene has zero draws; its build outcome can still match a successful reference build. The summary separately counts matching outcomes, drawable scenes and passing pictures. Those counts answer different questions. Some native analysis tests also expect separately generated fixtures under out and panic with a generation command if absent. A fresh checkout needs those generation steps for the comparison. My no-emit TypeScript typecheck passed. Two attempts to run a focused Bun suite exited 75 at the shared macOS test lock before executing assertions. I did not run the full suite, whose file and service tests create additional artifacts. The three successful renders above are independent observations. See `rust/crates/loft-gpu/tests/it/instances.rs:151`, `rust/crates/loft-core/tests/it/draws.rs:227`, `rust/crates/loft-core/tests/it/solve.rs:51`, `tools/parity/run.ts:209`, `tools/parity/run.ts:229` and `tests/setup.ts:11`.

I found a small, reproducible authoring bug with larger ownership implications. Create a parameter named gain with default 1 and range 0–2, then create it with the same name and default and range 0–10. In a fresh process I got the very same expression object with maximum 2 on the second call. The interning key excludes the range, and the viewer uses that range for its slider. Independently built scenes can therefore inherit the first declaration's UI. More broadly, the interning tries, scalar maps and recorder live at module scope and strongly retain entries. This speeds construction of repeated expressions. For an editor rebuilding many distinct designs I would measure retained memory over a long session. I observed the range collision; I did not measure a memory growth rate. The expensive duplication is semantic. Scalar operators have to agree across generated shaders, CPU evaluation, interval bounds, derivatives and the newer Rust tape. Some redundancy is indispensable for checking one implementation against another. Two artifact schemas, two Rust hosts and two distinct routes for turning the same scene into renderable instructions enlarge the set of places a change can be correct in isolation and wrong at the boundary. The TypeScript field shader even has separate color and depth march loops whose termination behavior must stay consistent. Among smaller leftovers, the internal `NO_SCENE` constant has no tracked TypeScript caller in the source, examples, tools or tests I searched; the recorder spells the same empty value inline. I would retire an unused constant readily. I would keep the older Rust loader while it connects an authoring path that works. ## What I would choose instead

For a browser product configurator with supplied models, I would choose three.js. The customer needs their existing asset on screen, materials preserved, and ordinary application controls around it. Loft's strongest examples start from formulas. Its export direction is useful: it can sample surfaces and meshes, mesh suitable fields, and produce skinned or morphed animation. The baker returns skip reasons for media and hit-only solids, and can refuse a motion approximation that misses tolerance. A GLB cannot be my fallback for preserving every procedural effect. I found no corresponding general asset import workflow at Loft's package entry. In a browser game where imported scenes, interaction and tooling dominate, I would choose Babylon. Loft's viewer provides orbiting, sliders, camera control and a live canvas; my team would own the editor and game systems around them. See `src/bake/mesh.ts:260`, `src/bake/motion.ts:52`, `src/bake/motion.ts:377`, `src/bake/gltf.ts:32`, `src/index.ts:3` and `viewer/main.ts:243`.

For a Rust game I would start with Bevy if I wanted entity lifetimes, scheduling and application structure, or raw wgpu if an existing renderer already owned those things. Loft's asset loader could live inside either choice if the procedural creature justified it. For one fullscreen mathematical effect, a Shadertoy-style fragment shader would give me a shorter route to the first pixel and an easier shader to inspect. That choice starts to cost me when a character needs an attachment point, intersections from a second camera, a synchronized warning shape and CPU queries. Conversely, raw wgpu lets me pick the attachment formats, pass order and instancing scheme for the entire game. In Loft's TypeScript interactive path the target must have the expected format and dimensions. A team should knowingly exchange some of that control for Loft's body coordinates, compile-time analysis and reusable style logic. See `src/host/host.ts:840`, `src/compile/context.ts:34`, `src/style.ts:207` and `tests/spacetime.test.ts:105`.

## Five things that would change my decision

1. **Render ordinary authored scenes through the new native path.** I would want the stock lantern, material spheres and titan to build with named cameras and nonempty draws, and to pass independently checked image and refusal comparisons. Make a missing expected frame fail the gate. This would change my Rust adoption decision more than another rendering technique. The relevant break is between `src/cli/main.ts:238` and `src/record/recorder.ts:132`.
2. **Ship a supported consumer interface.** Give me one versioned authoring package, an exported browser loader and a compatible native artifact/loader pair I can install in an empty project. Demonstrate a live frame, an offline build and loading that build without paths into this checkout. I would then consider budgeting Loft as a dependency rather than taking on a fork. The existing seams are visible in `package.json:7`, `src/core/native.ts:102` and `viewer/main.ts:7`.
3. **Publish representative frame and iteration budgets I can reproduce on target hardware.** For the titan, include a close moving camera, multiple characters, a volumetric overlap and a sustained camera path. Measure cold and warm load, median and tail frame latency, peak memory, and CPU/GPU time separately on a low-end target as well as a desktop. The normal-frame and attribution instruments already exist. My 540p best frames establish feasibility on one machine. See `src/cli/profile.ts:27` and `src/host/profile.ts:118`.
4. **Make repeated authoring and publishing predictable.** A scene should own its parameter metadata, a long edit session should have measurable memory bounds, and a reader should see a complete prior or new build during replacement. Preserve playback metadata across the file boundary. These changes would make me comfortable running an editor or asset service continuously. See `src/expr.ts:394`, `src/host/files.ts:34` and `src/cli/main.ts:104`.
5. **Prove an asset handoff in an independent application.** Let me put a Loft creature next to an imported character, share depth and camera, drive two copies with game state, read a contact or release point, and identify exactly which effects survive export. Use the same authored creature in the demonstration and an automated integration check. That would let me adopt Loft for one asset before deciding whether to give it more of the frame. The component pieces exist in `examples/bosses/titan/rig.ts:699`, `src/bake/mesh.ts:298` and `rust/crates/loft-gpu/src/lib.rs:1382`.

Today I would happily own the source for a procedural film whose creatures keep changing shape during production. For a general greenfield application, that same ownership would consume time I need for the application itself. The part worth bringing across the boundary is the authored body and everything that agrees about where it is.
