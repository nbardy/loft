# Motion side by side: hand keys or gait vs `solve` (19 Nov 2026)

The owner asked whether hand-written motion or `solve` gives more natural motion. After the scope change, this round
compares only the two existing methods. `spring` and the effort preference were not built, pending a design audit.
Each subject is shown with the same camera (`square`), the same 512² size and the same clock, as two panels side by
side: left (a) hand, right (b) solve.

## What each method is

**Hand (keys or gait).** Every curve is written directly as a function of time. For the heron, `keys` places a few
values per curve at the leap's beats: crouch at 0.35 s, takeoff at 0.6, the arc's top at 0.9, contact at 1.2, the
absorb at 1.4 and still at 1.8. `keys` joins them with a monotone cubic, so nothing overshoots a key. Body x, y and
pitch and the feet (x, y) each get 5 to 7 keys. Nothing checks that the flight is a parabola, that the legs are within
reach or that the feet clear the rail; the keys were placed so they do. For the crab (islandback's method), `gait`
plants the feet and swings them on arcs. The body rides the course frame at a fixed height, with a keyed bob
`0.09 cos(4πt/P)` and roll `0.04 sin(2πt/P)`, P = 2 s.

**Solve (least jerk).** Each unknown is a uniform cubic B-spline. The numbers minimise ∫ Σ (d³u/dt³)² dt subject to
the holds (augmented Lagrangian with Newton steps; `agent_notes/2026-11-18_solve/NOTE.md`). For the heron this is
heron_leap.ts unchanged. Its holds: standing still at 0, feet planted until 0.6, `accel = (0, -g)` from 0.6 to 1.2,
feet on the deck from 1.2, still at 1.8, legs within reach, and every part clear of the rail. **The crab had no solve
version; I wrote one** (`crab_walk_solve.ts`) with the existing API. The feet are the gait's, the same as the hand
panel. The body's carriage (x, height y, z, yaw) is 4 × spline(14) over 4 s, with these holds:
- the course's pose and speed at 0 s and at 4 s;
- every leg's root-to-foot distance between 0.5 and 0.95 of its span at all times.

## How each reads

**Heron.**
- **Hand:** reads as a cartoon jump. The crouch is where the animator put it (0.6 m down at 0.35 s). The flight is a
  monotone ease, not a parabola, so it hangs at the top and drops onto the deck. The body stays level and readable
  throughout.
- **Solve:**
  - The crouch is earned, not keyed: 0.77 m down at 0.40 s (CPU readout).
  - The flight is a true parabola, and the landing dips and recovers.
  - Over the rail, though, the bird pitches **67° nose-down at 0.925 s**, a dive that reads as wrong for a heron.
    Pitch is free and only clearance and jerk constrain it, so tipping the torso, neck and bill round the rail is the
    least-jerk way past.
  - The solve does what its holds say, physically right in the vertical and unnatural in attitude. A hold on pitch
    (e.g. `le(abs(c.pitch), 0.3)`) is the fix, at a cost of more authoring.

**Crab.**
- **Hand:** the keyed bob and roll give the body a small, regular pulse on the step.
- **Solve:** the solved carriage *is* the course. Measured on the CPU, the body is at most 3.7 mm off the arc, yaw
  within 5e-6 rad, and height constant at 2.50. No reach bound is active; the solve took 0.2 s.
- So the solve panel is the hand panel without the bob and roll: smoother and, because of that, deader. The feet step,
  but the body does not answer them.
- Least jerk over a fixed footing has no reason to move the body off a constant-speed path. Nothing in it carries
  weight, so the bob that loading would cause cannot appear.

## Verdict for this round

Neither method is natural on its own. Hand keys give the attitude and character but no physics. Least-jerk solve
gives correct ballistics and an earned anticipation where holds force motion (the heron's takeoff and landing). It
gives nothing where they do not (the crab's carriage), and it gives odd attitudes wherever a degree of freedom has no
hold.

The gap in both is the same: no mass and no load. That is what the deferred effort preference (load-dependent cost
would make the crab's body sink and shift onto its planted feet) and secondary springs were for. These two videos are
the baseline to judge them against.

Not measured: GPU frame time beyond the renderer's per-frame readout (about 2 to 3 ms at 512² for every panel; in both
methods the motion is a few per-frame scalars).
